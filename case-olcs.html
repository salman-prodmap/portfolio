From complex policy to a clearer service

Context

I led product ownership across three teams on the modernisation of OLCS, Ireland’s national platform for school staff appointments, leave and substitute claims. Serving 90,000+ teachers, approximately 4,000 schools and 20,000+ school users, the platform supports workflows that feed directly into payroll.

OLCS 2.0 was a risk-driven rebuild of a business-critical public-sector system, not a feature project.

The scale explains the stakes. The system held 7.6 million claims, 1.4 million leave records, 403,000 appointments and 181,000 staff records. All of it flows into teacher and SNA payroll. An error here isn’t a UI bug. It’s someone paid the wrong amount.

Working with Department of Education stakeholders and delivery partners, I translated undocumented business logic into clear requirements, strengthened delivery practices and simplified operational workflows. The programme went live in February 2026.

The business challenge

OLCS modernisation required reconstructing business rules spread across government circulars, legacy code and the knowledge of experienced staff. Incomplete requirements and fragmented delivery created dependencies, rework and uncertainty. Errors in appointments or leave claims could carry through to payroll, affecting schools and staff.

The hardest problems were not technical. They were about knowledge: what the old system actually did, and whether that was right. In January 2024 the programme went RED, and the stated causes were almost entirely requirements problems.

The programme needed a consistent way to establish the correct business behaviour, prepare work for development and coordinate delivery across teams.

Established a repeatable path from policy to delivery

I introduced a structured requirements pipeline combining stakeholder workshops, legacy-system testing, journey mapping and prototype reviews. Stories included business rules, exception scenarios, acceptance criteria and API definitions before entering development. I replaced separate front-end and back-end stories with complete user journeys, reducing hand-offs and making outcomes easier to test. Business-rule templates gained adoption across the programme.

Improved delivery flow and executive decision-making

Reporting to the EY Partner, I owned roadmap integrity, risk reporting and executive progress reviews. I overlaid progress, dependencies and risks on story maps, giving stakeholders a shared view of what needed resolving. Using delivery and flow metrics, I secured support for the programme’s transition from Scrum to Kanban. My responsibilities expanded to two additional teams, alongside repeated contract extensions.

Simplified workflows and strengthened payroll accuracy

Across appointments, claims, staff management and related modules, I challenged legacy behaviour and clarified ambiguous rules. Improvements included correcting appointment calculations that forced schools to re-enter data and redesigning orphaned claims so users could attach multiple claims to eligible leave records. I also led analysis of payroll file interfaces and worked with testing teams on end-to-end validation. Role-based access, auditability and data minimisation formed part of the requirements.

Key improvements

In total I identified and delivered 22 improvements over the legacy system, from input constraints that stop records being rejected downstream to screens that only ask what’s relevant and actions named by their outcome. Together they cut rework for schools, made delivery smoother across the programme and built the Department’s confidence in the new platform.

Orphaned claims, solved without changing business logic. A new idea: a visual display of the leave timeline and the claims available to attach, rather than manually attaching a single claim each time. It eliminated the risk of claims becoming orphaned, and contributed to the 1,000+ hours saved per month.

Applied AI to prevent avoidable errors

I shaped two AI initiatives that subsequently went live. The first used offline AI to map error messages to government circulars, with business and policy validation, so schools received clearer explanations. The second provided optional guidance during claim entry to flag potential conflicts across leave records. The early-warning pilot reduced claim rejections by approximately 50%.

Results and leadership impact

Delivery velocity increased from approximately 5 to 30 story points per sprint. Improvements across the delivered modules generated measured savings of over 1,000 operational hours per month.

  • 80% fewer defects than the parallel team.
  • Over half of claim-limit rules were unenforced in the legacy system; all are enforced in OLCS 2.0.
  • Led sprint reviews across 3 teams.
  • Led UAT across multiple teams.
  • Led complex integration of file transfers between payroll and leave system.
  • Four major modules delivered: Post-Primary Claims (DR scope), Orphaned Claims, Staff Management, most of the Family Leave catalogue.
  • Usability validated by schools in in-person demos.
  • Scope grew from one team to three, and my contract was extended several times on measurable impact.

The engagement demonstrates how I combine technical analysis with product judgment: turning complex policy into buildable requirements, aligning senior stakeholders and leading teams towards measurable service improvements.

Strategic insights

  • In legacy modernisation, the requirement is the risk. OLCS 2.0 went RED because of discovery, not code. When business logic is undocumented, analysis capacity sets delivery speed. Plan and staff it as a critical-path function, not as support to development.
  • “Like-for-like” is a trap. Legacy behaviour is evidence, not a specification. The claim-limit tests showed that faithfully copying OLCS 1 would have carried payroll errors into a brand-new platform. The right target is legacy behaviour, tested and then ratified by the business.
  • Visibility earns trust. One consistent voice for the team at fortnightly reviews, and demos of working software, kept Department stakeholders close to the detail. Positive feedback from five in-person school demos on navigation and usability then validated the design choices from outside the programme.
  • AI earns its place at the point of risk. In a regulated system, the best first use of Generative AI was not a chatbot. It was an early-warning check where errors are most expensive: leave submissions about to hit payroll.

Lessons learned

What worked, and I’d repeat:

  • Test the legacy system before writing the story. A structured set of scenarios run through OLCS 1 was worth more than weeks of assumption. It turned “how does it work?” into “here is what it does, and here is where it’s wrong”.
  • Specify the pattern once, then reuse it. Role matrices, worklist designs and status maps became templates. Every later module started halfway done.
  • Cut scope with evidence. Pausing unclear work and dropping unused features protected the team’s capacity for what mattered.

What I’d do differently:

  • Start integration analysis on day one. Map payroll files, status codes and data flows alongside screens, not after them.
  • Put accessibility into acceptance criteria from the first story. WCAG 2.1 AA and the European Accessibility Act became a formal test plan only in September 2025, so retrofitting cost more than building it in.
  • Push for measurable objectives early. The programme’s six objectives were qualitative. Targets for release frequency, defect rates and accessibility would have made success easier to evidence.
