From manual underwriting to a broker product

Context

Liberty Mutual is a Fortune 100 insurer with over $39 billion in annual revenue. Their equine and livestock division had a clear growth target: become a top 3 equine and livestock insurer in the US, providing what the team called a “best in show” customer experience.

When I joined, there was no digital product. Brokers were working across three legacy systems — OPUS, Salesforce, and Genius — and the end-to-end process from submission to policy issuance occupied almost a full clock face of processing time. The business had 146 booked policies in 2020 and wanted to reach 5,000+ active brokers and $50M annual GWP.

I joined approximately one month after discovery had completed, with one day of context handover. Discovery was performed by junior members of staff, on a set template.

The challenge

No digital product existed. The business was entirely on manual and legacy processes.

The underwriting rules were locked inside a complex Excel workbook. The development team was using Fire Flash to run Excel inside their code, which was a very slow process. The workbook was slow, expert-dependent, and impossible to implement in code as-is. Nobody had dared to open it and translate it properly. The combinations ran into the thousands, driven by horse age, sex, breed, use, state variations, coverage type, and subjectivities.

Only two underwriters in the entire business could perform complex premium calculations. Every other person was an assistant. Scaling to thousands of brokers was structurally impossible without solving this.

Engineering’s initial MVP proposal was to have underwriters manually enter the premium rate. Technically simpler. Commercially fatal.

Document generation — invoices, policy documents, payment schedules — was entirely manual.

The product also had to pass Liberty Mutual compliance standards, meet US state-level regulatory requirements, operate across devices, integrate with Auth0 for broker authentication, and connect with Genius for legacy data — all as a greenfield build.

Getting up to speed — and establishing credibility fast

I arrived one month post-discovery with minimal handover. I set myself one target for week one: write a single user story. I hit it. That small act of delivery signalled to the business that things were moving again. Heidi Philyaw, Director at Liberty Mutual, later wrote that I “joined about a month in, post discovery and framing, and didn’t miss a beat.”

Defining the vision and success metrics

Before writing a single user story for the team, I ran structured product visioning to align everyone on what success looked like. The output was a vision board that defined 8 user segments, user needs, business goals, product goals, product metrics, business metrics, and revenue streams — all on one page.

I then built a formal KPI framework with three categories:

  • Growth (agents onboarded, GWP, retention),
  • Frequency (policies created, quoted vs bound, new vs renewal), and
  • Efficiency (quote time, issue time, endorsement time — all measured per release).

North star metric: % of registered agents have written a policy without a 30-day gap.

This single number told us whether brokers were actively using the product or just registered on it.

Automated rating removed the two-expert bottleneck

The delivered MVP automated premium rating, allowing brokers to obtain quotes without waiting for either of the two specialist underwriters. Across the delivered portal, quote generation fell from 25 to 6 minutes and policy issuance from 52 to 13 minutes.

The initial engineering proposal required underwriters to enter premium rates manually. That would have left the business dependent on the same two experts, limiting its ability to grow towards the target of 5,000+ active brokers.

I used the business growth plan, underwriting training dependency and revenue projections to secure agreement that automated rating belonged in the MVP. Working with underwriters, I translated the complex rating workbook into decision tables that engineering could implement. The result was a working broker product with the rating knowledge built in, rather than another interface around a manual process.

The decision table work — translating the untranslatable

With engineering committed to automation, the problem was now mine to solve: how do you encode thousands of underwriting rules into something a developer can implement?

I took the Excel workbook apart completely. I analysed every combination of horse age, sex, breed, use, state variation, coverage type, and subjectivity condition. I then rebuilt the logic as decision tables — structured grids where every input combination maps to a defined output: a rate, an eligibility flag, or a triggered subjectivity requirement.

These decision tables were documented on the product Wiki and referenced directly from user stories, creating a traceable, auditable, and maintainable source of truth. No underwriter expertise required at runtime.

The result: brokers could now get an instant automated quote without any underwriter involvement whatsoever. This was the commercial unlock. It is what made 5,000+ brokers achievable.

Prioritising coverage by revenue, not complexity

With hundreds of possible coverage combinations to build, I prioritised by revenue potential and usage volume — not by technical ease.

The highest-revenue, highest-frequency coverages (mortality, major medical, less surgery) were built first. Lower-volume niche coverages came later.

This meant the product was generating real commercial value from the earliest possible release, not waiting for feature completeness.

User research and UX decisions

I ran formal user interviews and persona development with brokers and underwriters. The user research confirmed the two-expert bottleneck and shaped the automated rating decision. I also used empathy mapping and service design to define the full broker journey.

On UX, there was a genuine disagreement with the designer about the horse-entry flow: add all horses first, then return to add subjectivities — or add each horse with its full details before moving to the next. I favoured horse-by-horse. I backed the preference with A/B testing data from real brokers and underwriters. The horse-by-horse approach also handled US state variations more robustly at the point of data entry. The data settled it.

5-phase go-to-market plan

I defined the release and go-to-market strategy in five phases:

  • Phase 1: Registered pilot agents — view and download existing policies, full security stack (pen testing, threat modelling, Auth0 authentication)
  • Phase 2.1: Full quote, bind and issue, selected agents
  • Phase 2.2: Full quote, bind and issue, pay on issuance — all registered agent base
  • Phase 3: Insured self-service — view policies, pay my bill, access documents
  • Phase 4: New broker onboarding process
  • Phase 5: Wide market, advertising, farm product to direct insured channel

Formal usability testing

Before the broker release, I ran formal usability testing with 5 real agents using a high-fidelity prototype. Feedback was synthesised across four areas: app summary, documents, billing, and worklist. Unprompted reactions from the agents included: “Used other portals — love yours”, “Info is thorough, absolutely perfect”, “I absolutely see myself using this.”

Automation and document generation

Automated invoice generation, policy documents and payment schedules entirely, eliminating all manual document creation. Reduced document generation time by 98%.

There was a lot of concern around payment and billing, and the business wanted it to stay entirely manual, due to previous failed system integration attempts and stakeholder issues. However, we raised two concerns:

  • The chance of manual errors
  • Single-person dependency

We engaged the billing system (Genius) stakeholders early in the cycle to align with our backlog delivery, and we created prototypes and hackathons. The CIO took notice via the director and provided budget for Genius team resources to work with us on the integration.

Deliberate trade-off: I dropped automated monthly billing from the initial release. A half-baked version — manual input triggering auto document generation — would have shipped faster but set a poor precedent. Monthly billing happened post-issuance anyway. We shipped a clean product. By the time billing cycles hit, we already had the solution fully automated; we had carefully planned the transition from manual to automated billing.

The product was subsequently shared at internal tech talks, town halls, architecture collaboration sessions and external conferences. It was also featured in AWS’s official Liberty Mutual case study.

Outcomes

  • $5 million in gross written premium through the portal at time of CIO award (July 2021)
  • $1 million+ in new GWP from new strategic broker partnerships
  • 75% reduction in underwriting processing time — independently confirmed by CIO in company-wide recognition email
  • Policy issuance time reduced from 52 minutes to 13 minutes
  • Quote generation time reduced from 25 minutes to 6 minutes
  • 0-to-1 delivery in 24 weeks in a Fortune 100 regulated environment
  • Annual AWS infrastructure run cost of approximately $140 — enabled by serverless architecture decision
  • Team recognised as Exemplar team within Liberty IT, winning multiple internal awards
  • CIO Excellence Award Q2 2021 — James McGlennon, CIO Liberty Mutual, named the product in a company-wide email to all US Tech and LIT employees and partners
  • Featured in AWS’s official Liberty Mutual case study — external validation of technical and product quality

What I learned

Serverless was a real game changer. Running a production insurance portal for about $140 a year, with no servers to manage, meant the team’s time went into the product rather than the infrastructure.A well-run organisation makes you faster. Liberty already had processes in place, for example for security. Pen testing, threat modelling and authentication standards were ready to use, so we didn’t have to invent them, and that sped up development.In complex domains, the hard work is where the value is. Insurance rules are complicated, and the easy path was to automate the Excel workbook as it was. I challenged that. Working with the underwriters, I tore down a 20-year-old spreadsheet and defined 20+ rating tables, each with hundreds of entries, from the workbook and the underwriters’ own knowledge. That knowledge now runs in the product, and it’s what made instant quotes possible.
